# Threat to democracy and free speech in Germany

*Criminal law § 188, Trusted Flaggers and the online prosecution of citizens — a concise report*

---

## Introduction

For several years now, Germany has pursued a remarkably active policy of prosecuting citizens who insult or criticise politicians online. This approach rests on two pillars: a tightened criminal provision (§ 188 StGB) and an EU-established reporting system of so-called *Trusted Flaggers* under the Digital Services Act (DSA). Both instruments are legally grounded in themselves, but together — according to critics — they form an infrastructure that criminalises citizen criticism and increasingly puts pressure on freedom of expression.

## § 188 StGB: insulting politicians as a separate offence

§ 188 of the German Criminal Code makes insult, defamation and slander of persons "in political life" — from the federal chancellor to local councillors — punishable by up to three years' imprisonment for insult and up to five years for slander.[^1] In 2021 the provision was broadened: ordinary "insult" (not just slander) now falls under it, and online expressions were explicitly included.[^2]

The consequences are visible in the statistics. According to the *Bundeskriminalamt*, the number of § 188 cases referred to the justice system rose from 468 in the first quarter of 2024 to 1,690 in the first quarter of 2025 — almost a fourfold increase in a single year. The total number of police registrations grew from 1,404 (2022) to 4,439 (2024).[^3] Once a year, the German federal police carry out a coordinated "action day": on 25 June 2025, house searches were conducted on 170 citizens simultaneously and devices were seized over online statements.[^4]

High-profile complainants are not rare. Former vice-chancellor Robert Habeck (Greens) filed more than 800 criminal complaints against citizens over online insults; former minister Annalena Baerbock around 513; AfD leader Alice Weidel and the current federal chancellor Friedrich Merz also filed hundreds of complaints.[^5] Notorious is the case of the 64-year-old pensioner who was convicted for calling Habeck "Schwachkopf" (idiot) on X — followed by a house search at dawn.[^2]

## Trusted Flaggers under the DSA

Article 22 of the EU Digital Services Act creates *Trusted Flaggers*: organisations designated by the national coordinator (in Germany the Bundesnetzagentur) whose reports must be treated with priority by platforms. Platforms formally decide on removal themselves; Trusted Flaggers have no power to enforce it.[^6]

Germany is one of the few member states to have the system fully operational. The first — and for a long time only — recognised Trusted Flagger is **REspect!**, the reporting office of the Youth Foundation of Baden-Württemberg. **HateAid** operates in the same field. REspect! assesses reports, forwards content deemed criminal to the Bundeskriminalamt and a specialised prosecutor's office, and then informs the platform.[^7] A report to a Trusted Flagger in Germany therefore simultaneously acts as an entry point into a criminal proceeding — a step the DSA itself does not prescribe.

## Where the threat lies

- **Independence in question.** Research cited by the CADE Project and Euronews shows that REspect! receives roughly 95% of its funding from the federal programme *Demokratie leben!*. The DSA requires independence from platforms, but not explicitly from the government, which itself has an interest in § 188 enforcement.[^8]
- **Shifting definitions.** The Bundesnetzagentur initially spoke of removing "hate and fake news" and had to correct itself after public criticism to "illegal hate and illegal fake news" — hate and falsehood are not automatically criminal.[^7]
- **Chilling effect.** Courts later declared house searches in high-profile cases unlawful, but victims received no compensation. The threshold for contradicting politicians online is thereby structurally raised.[^4]
- **Institutional bias.** The Yale Information Society Project warns that Trusted Flagger systems, through their priority treatment, structurally tend toward over-removal and thus against free expression.[^9]
- **Reasonable counter-voice rejected.** In 2025/2026 the Bundestag rejected an AfD proposal to abolish § 188; the provision, called a "muzzle law" by critics, remains fully in force.[^3]

## What it means for democracy and free speech

A democracy assumes that citizens can criticise office-holders unsparingly — even rudely. § 188, by contrast, elevates politicians to a protected category with heavier maximum penalties than ordinary citizens. Combined with a reporting chain in which a government-funded NGO passes reports directly to police and prosecutor, and platforms are under DSA pressure to remove quickly, a system emerges in which the costs of political criticism (house search, seizure, fine, legal assistance) fall asymmetrically on the citizen. Even when courts later correct course, the deterrent effect has already occurred. The problem is therefore not one law or one NGO, but the *combination*: a broad criminal provision, a centralised reporting system and a platform obligation to give priority treatment. That is precisely the constellation that journalists, jurists and civil-rights organisations — on both the left and the right — warn about.[^5] [^9]

## Conclusion

Germany's approach to online insult illustrates how well-intentioned instruments against hate speech can evolve into an infrastructure that hits critical citizens. As long as § 188 remains unchanged, Trusted Flaggers are closely intertwined with law-enforcement agencies, and transparency about funding and criteria remains limited, the system remains a real pressure factor on public discussion. The European Commission's upcoming guidelines on Article 22 DSA, and the call for a strict separation between content moderation and criminal prosecution, are therefore of essential importance to the health of European public debate.
