# Stop verifying age. Start labelling content and enforce it.

### A simpler way to give parents the control they need

**A personal working document. This is not an official Kids Unplugged viewpoint.**

---
## Introduction

This proposal is a privacy-friendly, cybersecurity-approved way to improve and harmonize parental controls without the drawbacks of age gating performed by government or private companies. It builds on existing concepts: age ratings, content labelling, app store labels, parental controls. It was inspired by the American [California AB 1043](https://www.alstonprivacy.com/california-enacts-digital-age-verification-law/) and the federal [App Store Accountability Act](https://james.house.gov/news/documentsingle.aspx?DocumentID=247) ("Parents Decide"-style bills where devices know and enforce the age of a user) but it does not entail a mandatory verified age flag. It's also more privacy-friendly than even the [EU Mini-Wallet](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/04/age-verification-european-union-mini-id-wallet). It was further inspired by the excellent work of [Balance Phone](https://www.thebalancephone.com/). The proposed approach flips the burden: instead of telling websites how old we are, websites tell us what their content is (heavily audited), and our devices then decide whether to block them or not. Indeed, the most private way of blocking content is at the device level. This requires two significant changes. The first is a harmonized way for content providers to attach age and content labels which in many cases already exists. The second is to extend existing parental controls with the ability to read and enforce those labels.

## Parents already have controls, but they're exhausting and leaking

Most parents use parental controls. Screen-time limits, app blockers, kid accounts and content filters are built into most phones, consoles and operating systems. Millions of families rely on them. In fact, I don't know any parent handing a device to a child without at least a form of parental controls, except when it's their own device with their own accounts. Even age gating wouldn't do anything in that case.

Parental controls usually work by **blacklisting or whitelisting**: a cumbersome process where you block bad apps or websites one by one, or allow good ones one by one, optionally with a time limit of some sort. The list never stops growing. A new game appears, a friend recommends an app, a website changes overnight, and you're back to deciding. It's a part-time job that never ends, and you're doing it half-blind.

## The questions parents pose all the time

At every information evening about kids and screens, the same two questions come up first:

- **"From what age is *this* app or game okay?"**
- **"What are the actual dangers in it?"**

Such information does exist, but it's **scattered everywhere, duplicated, incoherent, sometimes locked behind paywalls, sometimes buried in reviews, often biased**

Age verification, the model governments are rushing toward, does **nothing** for this. Even if your device perfectly knew your child was 11, it still couldn't tell you whether a given app suits a 11-year-old. The current proposals are aimed almost entirely at **social media** — the EU Parliament, for example, has [backed a minimum age of 16 for social media, video platforms and AI companions (with access from 13 allowed where there is parental consent)](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20251120IPR31496/children-should-be-at-least-16-to-access-social-media-say-meps) — while leaving out whole categories of addictive and harmful content like **video games (first and foremost)**, gambling, "nudify" apps, and more.

## Age labels should be made abundant, easy and apply automatically

Instead of making kids prove their age to every app, make **every app, website and platform declare what's inside it**: a clear age rating plus plain content warnings (violence, stranger chat, nudity, gambling-style mechanics, and so on). Not every app or website, of course — the effort applies mostly to big platforms and those whose content could be harmful.

Firstly, this works preferably **per item**, not just per app. Roblox, for example, is millions of games, so the label lives **per game** — and per video, per piece of content. That's already how it works for films and TV. In case this is not possible, the content labels and age ratings do need to  apply to the whole app or the whole platform.

Secondly, this labelling should be **heavily verified and audited** by relevant institutions. Roblox now tags pornographic or fascist content as 'all ages'. If platforms like Roblox don't change their ways, a blanket label 16+ should be forced upon them. In fact, this is the second way to go about content labelling: **alternatively, let there be an external database of content labels, managed by an authority or institution.**

Thirdly, the **labelling is for 'worst setting'.** The chat functionality in Roblox or other games can be turned off or on depending on the parental settings. Parents shouldn't have to bother with that. **The platform should be safe by design, not by setting.** The possibility of chat alone is enough to get that content label and increase the age recommendation, period. If Roblox wants a lower age recommendation, they'll need to bring out a version where chat is not possible at all.

## How it would work in practice


1. **The content carries a label** Each app, website, game or video ships a standard, machine-readable age rating and content tags — the same idea as [PEGI](https://pegi.info/page/pegi-age-ratings), just digital and automatic. Roblox tags each game; YouTube tags each video; a website declares it in its page. The label lives on the respective platform or website (app store, game store, and so on). **In absence of labels, or as an alternative, centralized list can suffice** as said before. Balance Phone is an example: it uses its own labelling.
2. **The device holds the rules.** Parental controls are expanded and simplified in use: you tell your child's phone, computer, tablet or console "this account is for a 14-year-old," and the age never leaves the device. Because the device knows which labels to allow and which to block, it does so automatically, for everything — no more maintaining lists app by app. This applies automatically and by default. **The usual parental control settings and screen-time limits remain possible**, of course. Governments can suggest a recommended default by specifying the labels ("violent games 16+", "social media 15+"). And instead of the default labelling, you can **import a policy from a third party you trust more**: a pediatric association, a school network, a parenting organisation, a faith community,... and let their recommendations override it where applicable. This also means a significant technical change for parental controls and account management: one consequence is that parental controls no longer stop at 13. Because the device, not the platform, enforces the rules, a parent can keep meaningful, age-appropriate controls in place for a 15- or 17-year-old, rather than losing all leverage the moment a child is old enough to open accounts on their own. As for desktops: Windows and Mac parental controls would need to be much more tight than they are now (where installs from outside the app store stay outside the scope, this needs to change, too).
3. **The device enforces it locally — no data leaves the device, and VPNs are not a loophole.** When your child opens something, the device checks the label against your rules and allows or blocks it, on device. The app is never told how old your child is. No age, no ID, no data leaves the device at all. VPNs don't offer a way out, because the device simply reads the age or content label. A good example is [Balance Phone](https://www.thebalancephone.com/), which takes a rudimentary yet effective approach: everything with a certain category (e.g. video, gaming or social media) is blocked device-wide, for everyone.
4. **One single parental control** As stated above: the parental controls are simply managed by the OS and uses the age the OS knows. Platforms need to allow this, or they get the highest age rating. E.g.: if Roblox does not allow the OS to filter the games, than the whole of Roblox gets the highest age rating. The point is NOT that parents have to configure parental controls within each platform separately. That would be too easy of a loophole for platforms tocircumvent the OS, get the real age of a user, and confuse parents with complicated parental controls. Because:

The key difference from age verification is that **the label flows into a device; your child's age never flows out**.

**Some of the same loopholes as with age verification remain, though**: sharing credentials, sharing accounts, sharing devices.

One privacy issue also remains: you still share the age (or an approximate age) with the device, and therefore possibly with the online account tied to it. I.e. Apple, Google or Microsoft will know the approximate age. If for privacy reasons, or other reasons, parents choose to not apply parental controls or their kids do not use a device which has parental controls (Linux comes to mind), content labels can still provide useful guidelines. **Parental controls or selective blocking based on labels can easily be applied on the router level, as a browser extension, as a preset for a home network offered by the ISP, and the like.**

# This addresses the main criticisms of age verification

## It puts the effort where it belongs

**Age verification blames the victims.** It makes every child and every adult repeatedly prove themselves, carry the burden of identification, and bear the risk when their data leaks. The people being protected are the ones forced to jump through hoops. Meanwhile the platforms whose addictive, harmful design caused the worry in the first place — social media, games — don't have to change a thing.

**Labelling flips the responsibility onto the perpetrators.** The effort lands on the platforms: *they* must declare what's in their product, honestly, and answer for it when they lie. That's exactly where it should be. Device manufacturers, who provide the gateway to content and are de facto part of the problem, must also do some efforts. The ones causing the problem do the work. 

## It puts parents in charge, not the government

A common and fair objection to age verification is: *these decisions belong with parents, not the state.* Age verification gets this backwards: the government and the platforms decide what your child can reach, and you're left trusting their goodwill or competence. We've seen this fail invariantly. Take the EU: not only is their proposed solution lacking technically (see further, and [Age Verification in Europe](age_verification_europe_report.md) for the technical details), it's also very weak in policy (see earlier): the EU basically proposed 13 for social media, video and companions and leaves other content unaffected. The EU would force upon its population a mass-surveillance mechanism with enormous cybersecurity risks for all parties involved, for little to no benefit at all.

Labelling does the opposite: it hands the power to parents and gives them a baseline. The government's role remains at defining how content gets labelled and, crucially, making sure it's respected and audited.

It's not the scope of this document, but it needs to be said that a technological solution is not enough. The social norm needs to change, too!

## It avoids a serious cybersecurity problem

The main reason to prefer labelling content over verifying kids is the one people seem to understand the least: **age verification is dangerous even when it's done well.**

The instinct is to say "fine, but the EU's version is privacy-friendly — it just proves you're over 16 without revealing who you are." On paper, yes. In practice, the picture is worse:

- **The systems leak.** To check ages, companies collect IDs, selfies and biometric data — and that data gets breached. A Discord verification vendor exposed roughly [70,000 government IDs and selfies](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/04/digital-identities-and-future-age-verification-europe) in 2025. The only data that can't leak is data that was never collected.
- **Even the "good" EU app failed.** The Commission's own privacy-preserving age-verification app was [bypassed in under two minutes](https://proton.me/blog/eu-age-verification-app-hacked) the day after launch — by flipping a single setting in a file — and was found [storing facial images read from ID documents as unencrypted files and never deleting the selfies it wrote to disk](https://idtechwire.com/researcher-finds-security-flaws-in-eu-age-verification-app/). "Highest privacy standards in the world" turned out to be a mirage.
- **It's not zero-knowledge**. (Zero knowledge means: app/website doesn't know user, and age verification app doesn't know website). In the EU blueprint, zero-knowledge proofs are ["should implement", not "must implement"](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/04/age-verification-european-union-mini-id-wallet), so a national version could legally ship as merely pseudonymous. Apps built on national eID (e.g. [Itsme](https://www.itsme-id.com) when extended for this purpose) can log when they are used and for what: Itsme's own [privacy policy](https://www.itsme-id.com/legal/app-privacy-policy) states it keeps a record of the transactions you did or did not confirm for up to 10 years after your last use. Identity is hidden from the website, but not from the age verification app and the state.
- **It locks out ordinary people.** The shipped EU system leans on Google and Apple phone-attestation and a [national eID or smartphone many people don't have](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2025/04/age-verification-european-union-mini-id-wallet). Not only is this a problem for European digital sovereignty, it's also discriminatory: it excludes Linux users, de-Googled phones, people without a smartphone, and adults without a national digital ID. First-class citizens turned into second-class digital ones.

### The slippery slope is the real danger

The deeper problem is what age verification becomes. Once you've built infrastructure that identifies who is doing what online, pressure mounts to use it for more.

- **VPN crackdowns.** Kids bypass age checks with VPNs, so officials start calling VPNs a "loophole to close." The UK House of Lords already [voted 207 to 159 to ban VPNs for under-18s](https://www.techradar.com/vpn/vpn-privacy-security/uk-government-targets-vpns-in-new-online-safety-consultation-as-lords-vote-for-ban), and Utah [wrote VPNs into its age-verification law](https://www.eff.org/deeplinks/2026/04/utahs-new-law-regulating-vpns-goes-effect-next-week) — the first US state to do so. But a VPN isn't a kids' trick, it's a basic cybersecurity necessity that millions of people rely on every day to secure their connection on public Wi-Fi and to stop advertisers, networks and other third parties from tracking what they do online. And beyond advertisers, a VPN also offers protection against governments. VPNs don't just protect journalists, activists and abuse survivors; they protect everyone, in the future as much as now. We just watched a long-standing democracy, the United States, swing to a leader who began [stripping legal status from over 1.6 million people, deporting some in defiance of court orders, and using indiscriminate and at times lethal force](https://www.americanprogress.org/article/the-trump-administrations-assault-on-immigrants-degrades-the-rule-of-law/). [LGBTQ people fear they are next](https://williamsinstitute.law.ucla.edu/publications/impact-mass-deportations-lgbt/). With a record of who visited which sites, a government can simply look up who browsed, say, LGBTQ websites, **including years before anyone became a target.** This is why privacy needs to be protected even in peaceful times: the browsing history you leave today can be turned against you by whoever holds power tomorrow. And this is not only an American risk. The 2026 [V-Dem Democracy Report](https://www.gu.se/en/news/democratic-backsliding-reaches-western-democracies-with-us-decline-unprecedented) finds that six of the ten newest cases of democratic backsliding are in Europe and North America (naming established democracies like Italy and the United Kingdom) while inside the EU itself, governments in Hungary and Slovakia have [captured courts, media and anti-corruption bodies](https://cepa.org/article/europes-democratic-backsliding-is-spreading-like-malware/). Europe is sliding too (Germany's prosecution of online critics is a case in point — see [Threat to democracy and free speech in Germany](rapport_duitsland_meningsuiting_EN.md)), so building surveillance infrastructure in the EU is no safer.
- **Chat Control.** A [parallel EU proposal](https://edri.org/our-work/chat-control-what-is-actually-going-on/) would scan the images on your own device (before they're encrypted) to check whether they contain child sexual abuse material. It shares the same core premise as on-device age checks: that your device should act as a checkpoint reporting on you. The slippery slope is twofold. First, once the scanning machinery exists, it can be quietly extended to all kinds of other content at will and even to your text messages (to see whether you talk bad about the government, for instance). Secondly, [flagged images are forwarded to human reviewers](https://volteuropa.org/news/chat-control-wont-protect-children), and because the AI cannot tell innocent nudity apart from abuse (a teenager's bikini holiday photo, a nude shared between teenage partners, a skin condition sent to a doctor) [a large share of what gets sent off is perfectly innocent](https://www.mpg.de/25788438/chat-control-eu-client-side-scanning). Your most private and sensitive photos, which are most probably *not* abuse material, get leaked to strangers in a government building. 
- **"Going dark" and weaker encryption.** The same momentum pushes toward giving authorities access to encrypted data, weakening the protection that keeps everyone's banking, messages and photos safe.

**Content labelling sidesteps all of this.** Because no one is ever identified and nothing leaves the device, there's no honeypot of IDs to breach, no "VPN loophole" to close, and no surveillance infrastructure to repurpose later. It protects kids from social media and games now, puts the parents in control, but also gives kids a democracy to live in later.

## It's a real workload — but one we're paying for anyway

Labelling everything is a big job, and it has to be **audited or performed by the relevant institutions**, with real penalties for platforms that lie (calling adult content "all ages" to widen their audience).

But notice: age verification is *also* an enormous workload: building age-check systems, ID infrastructure, age-signal APIs across every operating system and app store. The effort happens either way. The difference is **where it goes**: into surveilling every child, or into honestly labelling content **once**, so everyone benefits forever. It's not a risky overhaul of internet infrastructure just to block social media only.

**Labelling is a known system. Everyone already knows [PEGI ratings](https://pegi.info/page/pegi-age-ratings) on games and the age labels on films, or ecolabels, or [Nutri-Score](https://www.bmleh.de/EN/topics/food-and-nutrition/food-labelling/extended-nutritional-labelling-system-nutri-score.html). Most people find them useful at least in some aspects.** Parents read them every day, and a huge amount of the rating work has already been done. We're not inventing a system from scratch; we're taking one people already understand and extending it to apps, websites and platforms covering games and everything else, not just social media.
